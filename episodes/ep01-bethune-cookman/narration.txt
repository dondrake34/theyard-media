October third, nineteen-oh-four. Daytona Beach, Florida. A young woman unlocks the door of a small rented house, right next to the city dump. Inside, the desks are old wooden crates. And in her pocket? One dollar and fifty cents.

Her name was Mary McLeod Bethune. That morning, five little girls walked through that door, along with her own young son, Albert. Nobody in that room knew what they were starting. Because that tiny school would one day become an H. B. C. U. A university that still carries her name. So how do you build a college out of a dollar fifty?

It starts in eighteen seventy-five, in Mayesville, South Carolina. Mary was the fifteenth of seventeen children. Her parents had been enslaved. She was the only child in her family to go to school, and she walked five miles a day to get there. Every evening, she came home and taught her family what she had learned. Teaching wasn't her job yet. It was already her mission.

Years later, in Daytona, she had almost nothing. No money for supplies. So she made them. Ink? Squeezed from elderberries. Pencils? Slivers of burned wood. Desks? Crates that other people threw away. And when the school needed money, she baked sweet potato pies, and she sold them. But pies only stretch so far. And the girls kept coming.

Within two years, her school had grown from six students to two hundred and fifty. So she knocked on doors. She wrote letters. She asked wealthy winter visitors to invest in her girls, including James Gamble, of the soap company Procter and Gamble. Some said no. She kept asking.

In nineteen twenty-three, her school merged with the Cookman Institute, a school for boys in Jacksonville, founded back in eighteen seventy-two. Girls and boys, learning side by side. And in nineteen thirty-one, the school took a new name. Bethune-Cookman College.

But here's the part most people never hear. Mary McLeod Bethune didn't stop at Daytona. She advised President Franklin D. Roosevelt. She founded the National Council of Negro Women. And in July of twenty twenty-two, a marble statue of her was unveiled inside the United States Capitol, in National Statuary Hall. She became the first Black American in that collection. Her statue replaced one of a Confederate general.

From a little house by the dump, to the halls of Congress. And all of it started with a dollar fifty.

Today, students walk the campus of Bethune-Cookman University under a simple motto. Enter to learn. Depart to serve. So the next time someone tells you that you don't have enough to start, remember Mary.
